# Long-form publication: a decision log

## Source

~~~text
Every team has a drawer full of tiny decisions. This is a universal truth in the modern workplace, and it is more important than ever in an era of rapidly evolving tools and distributed collaboration. We decide whether a timestamp means creation or receipt, whether “archive” hides a record or freezes it, and whether a retry button repeats the last step or starts the whole job again. Each choice looks small. Together, they become the shape of the product.

The interesting thing is that these decisions rarely arrive wearing decision-shaped hats. They arrive as a question in chat, a comment at the bottom of a pull request, or a sentence someone adds to a ticket before lunch. Because they appear informal, we answer them informally. “Use local time for now.” “Archive is basically delete, but safer.” “Retry the whole thing.” Six months later, those scraps are doing policy work.

This is not merely a documentation problem; it is a memory problem, a coordination problem, and ultimately a trust problem. When the reasoning disappears, the next person sees only the behavior. They can preserve it without understanding it, or change it without seeing who relied on it. Neither option is especially good, and both options can create significant downstream challenges.

I started keeping a decision log after we spent a rainy Tuesday arguing about a grey button. The button was not the real disagreement. One person thought “disabled” meant temporarily unavailable; another thought it meant the feature did not apply. The earlier discussion had happened in a video call and vanished with it. We reconstructed the choice from a screenshot, two half-remembered sentences, and the fact that nobody wanted to schedule another meeting. Glamorous work, this was not.

My log is deliberately plain: date, decision, reason, owner, and a link to the place where the change landed. If we considered an alternative, I add one line about why we did not choose it. That is all. The goal is not to create a comprehensive and exhaustive repository of organizational knowledge. The goal is to leave enough thread for someone else to pull.

It is important to note that a decision log can also become theatre. A template grows fields because fields are easy to add. Soon every choice asks for impact scores, stakeholder matrices, and a paragraph on strategic alignment. People stop writing entries, or they write fog. A blank field at least tells the truth.

The useful test is simple: could a person who was not in the room explain why the choice made sense at the time? Not whether it was perfect. Not whether we would make it again. At the time. That last phrase matters because decisions age in context: the customer was waiting, the API had a limit, the team had three days, the supposedly temporary workaround was the only bridge that reached the other bank.

In conclusion, teams should embrace lightweight decision logging as a powerful and transformative practice. It is not about creating more process; it is about creating more clarity. It is not about recording everything; it is about remembering what matters. The best decision log is not the longest one. It is the one that saves a future teammate from holding an archaeological dig in chat history.
~~~

## Assertions

- **Fidelity:** Retain the thesis that informal small decisions accumulate into product behavior; the three product examples; the six-month consequence; the grey-button anecdote and competing meanings; the lost video-call context and reconstruction; the log fields and rejected-alternative note; the anti-bureaucracy caveat; and the “made sense at the time” test with its customer, API, three-day, and workaround constraints.
- **Naturalness:** Remove or compress generic era-setting, meta-signposts, canned contrast formulas, repetitive conclusion language, and corporate intensifiers while preserving genuine paragraph boundaries and varied sentence lengths.
- **Register:** Read as a reflective, publication-ready workplace/technology essay, not a chat message, academic paper, leadership memo, or listicle.
- **Voice preservation:** Keep a clear majority of the distinctive drawer, decision-shaped hats, rainy Tuesday, dry “Glamorous work” aside, thread, “write fog,” repeated “At the time,” bridge, and archaeological imagery. Preserve `grey` and purposeful short fragments rather than replacing them with generic polish.
- **Non-invention:** Add no research findings, metrics, company details, compliance rationale, new meeting outcome, product incident, author credentials, or claim that decision logs prevent bad decisions.

## Common failures

- Flatten the concrete anecdote, dry humor, British spelling, or varied cadence.
- Treat all imagery as decoration while leaving generic corporate scaffolding intact.
- Turn the essay into a listicle, policy memo, or universal prescription.
- Drop the warning that decision logs can become performative bureaucracy.
- Add studies, metrics, organizational history, or personal biography.
