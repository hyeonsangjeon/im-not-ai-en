# Sentence-level copyediting

Use this guide when the source contains awkward or non-native English, when a structural edit has changed sentence grammar, or when a local repair is not obvious. Aim for idiomatic prose that still belongs to the writer. Do not use surface correctness to justify a change in meaning.

## Choose the smallest safe intervention

Classify a suspected issue before editing it:

- **Correct:** Fix an unambiguous error locally.
- **Recast:** Rewrite the smallest clause when the meaning is clear but a word-for-word repair would remain unnatural.
- **Leave:** Preserve an accepted dialect, register, house-style, or pronunciation variant.
- **Flag:** Preserve and briefly note wording that has more than one material reading.

Treat a clean sentence as a valid result. Do not replace it merely to demonstrate that editing occurred.

## Work in order

1. Mark protected literals, terminology, quotations, and functional structure.
2. Infer the dominant variety of English and the source's register.
3. Repair the sentence skeleton: boundaries, predicates, agreement, tense, and clause attachment.
4. Repair noun phrases: articles, determiners, number, and countability.
5. Resolve pronouns and modifiers only when the intended attachment is supported by the source.
6. Repair clear usage, collocation, spelling, apostrophe, and punctuation errors.
7. Read the sentence with its neighbours and restore any lost emphasis, cadence, or voice marker.

## Articles and determiners

- Judge `a` and `an` by the following sound, not the first written letter. Account for abbreviations, numbers, and genuine pronunciation variants.
- Distinguish a new countable item, a specific or previously identified item, and a general category before choosing `a`, `the`, or no article.
- Repair a missing determiner on a singular count noun only when the intended reference is clear.
- Do not add specificity by changing a general reference to `the`, or remove specificity by changing `the` to a generic form.
- Preserve established article use in product names, headings, code-adjacent labels, and domain terminology.

## Number, countability, and agreement

- Make subjects and finite verbs agree after locating the real head of the subject, not merely the nearest noun.
- Check coordinated subjects, quantities, collective nouns, relative clauses, and `each` or `every` constructions in context.
- Keep count and mass meanings distinct. Repair an impossible plural or missing unit only when the source supplies the intended quantity or category.
- Make pronouns agree with their supported antecedents while preserving a writer's natural singular `they` and the document's dialect conventions.
- Do not rewrite a domain term because its grammatical number looks unusual in general English.

## Reference, attachment, and time

- Replace a vague pronoun with an existing noun only when the surrounding text identifies that noun. Otherwise flag the ambiguity.
- Repair a dangling or misplaced modifier without inventing an actor. Use a passive or impersonal construction when the source does not establish agency.
- Preserve tense, aspect, conditionals, negation, and modal force. These encode chronology, completion, possibility, obligation, and confidence, not just grammar.
- Recheck antecedents, callbacks, and time relationships after splitting, combining, or moving sentences.

## Prepositions, collocations, and word choice

- Prefer the established combination of verb, adjective, noun, and preposition when it expresses the source meaning unambiguously.
- Recast locally when a literal or translated construction is grammatical in pieces but not idiomatic as a whole.
- Give field-specific usage priority over a general-language preference. Keep stable terminology stable.
- Do not use a synonym merely because a word repeats. Necessary repetition often improves technical clarity; deliberate repetition can carry voice or emphasis.
- Treat adjacent duplicate words as an error only after ruling out a valid construction, a name, quoted material, or deliberate emphasis.

## Contractions, punctuation, and spelling

- Correct a missing or misplaced apostrophe without deciding that every contraction should be expanded or introduced.
- Let register and the writer's existing habits determine whether contractions belong. Do not introduce or expand them only to make prose seem more or less conversational.
- Repair genuine sentence fragments, run-ons, and comma splices; preserve fragments that clearly function as deliberate emphasis or conversational rhythm.
- Follow the source or stated house style for serial commas, quotation marks, dashes, and similar choices. Do not treat a preferred style as universal grammar or globally convert straight and curly quotation marks or apostrophes.
- Preserve the dominant US, UK, or other established variety. Several consistent spelling, vocabulary, or agreement cues can establish that variety; repair isolated conflicting forms when the evidence is clear. Do not infer a document-wide conversion from a single cue.

## Protect technical and quoted material

Exclude code, identifiers, commands, URLs, link targets, citations, equations, logs, and quoted input from ordinary copyediting. Correct surrounding prose without altering those spans. Do not treat an unfamiliar technical token as a typo or silently expand an abbreviation.

## Read for a human result

Correctness is the floor, not the voice. After the copyedit, read the passage aloud mentally:

- keep natural variation in sentence length and opening;
- keep purposeful bluntness, warmth, restraint, humour, or formality;
- remove stiffness created by literal repair, but do not decorate the prose;
- leave already natural passages unchanged;
- make sure the result sounds like the same writer with fewer obstacles, not a house-style template.

Before returning, review every changed span. Revert any edit that only substitutes an equally valid contraction, article, punctuation mark, spelling, or phrase and solves no observed problem.
