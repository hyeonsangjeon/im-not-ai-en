# Editorial guide

Read this guide for standard and long-form work, or when the source's register is hard to infer. Use it as a diagnostic aid, not a list of forbidden forms.

## Diagnose the passage

Focus on patterns that repeat, cluster, obscure the point, or clash with the source. Leave isolated natural uses alone.

### Empty framing and redundant guidance

- Generic scene-setting delays the actual subject. Phrases such as “At a high level” are empty when no change in level or scope follows.
- Section previews, recaps, and conclusion announcements repeat information the structure already makes clear.
- Transitions label obvious relationships instead of expressing them.
- Sentences such as “This highlights” or “It is important to note” add no meaning.

Repair by opening on the concrete claim, request, event, or observation. Keep navigation that genuinely helps readers move through a long or procedural document.

### Manufactured rhetoric

- Repeated `not X but Y`, `more than X`, question-and-answer hooks, triads, or mirrored pairs make every idea land the same way.
- Fake quotations turn ordinary concepts into slogans or labels.
- Every paragraph resolves into a summary, takeaway, or punchline.
- Em dashes, fragments, or one-line paragraphs are added solely for drama.

Keep the strongest useful instance. Rewrite the rest according to their real logical relationship. Preserve deliberate rhetoric that belongs to the writer or genre.

### Decorative symbol scaffolding

- Repeated `label: explanation` frames make ordinary prose read like slide notes or generated summaries.
- Backticks around non-code words manufacture emphasis instead of earning it through wording or placement.
- Hyphens, en dashes, or em dashes become mechanical when they repeatedly bridge thoughts that would read more naturally as sentences or clauses.

Prefer a clear subject and verb, an ordinary sentence boundary, a conjunction or transition supported by the source, or a paragraph break at a genuine thought boundary. Do not replace a dash with `because`, `therefore`, or another logical connector unless the source actually supports that relationship.

Do not simply trade one device for another. Parentheses, semicolons, headings, fragments, and transition phrases are not automatic substitutes. Use them only when they fit the source, grammar, and register.

This is not a character ban. Preserve the writer's natural punctuation and functional syntax, including lexical hyphens, ranges, Markdown, code spans, identifiers, command flags, URLs, times, ratios, citations, interface labels, and stated house style.

### Vague importance and hidden agency

- Generic praise such as “robust,” “seamless,” or “transformative” stands in for an observable property.
- Abstract subjects and light-verb constructions hide who did what.
- Importance claims do not say what succeeds, fails, changes, or depends on the point.
- Stacked hedges blur the intended degree of uncertainty.

Name the actor and action only when the source supports them. State an existing consequence rather than inventing one. Reduce a hedge only when it is genuinely redundant. Combinations such as “should perhaps consider” can encode both recommendation strength and tentativeness; preserve that force even if the wording changes. Preserve technical meanings such as robust statistics and terms of art.

### Flattened voice and mechanical rhythm

- A run of sentences shares the same length, opening, or clause pattern without a rhetorical purpose.
- Paragraphs have identical shapes regardless of changes in idea, actor, time, evidence, or consequence.
- Editing would replace an author's plain, blunt, warm, skeptical, dry, or playful choices with neutral polish.
- Contractions, slang, fragments, or punctuation are inserted uniformly to simulate spontaneity.

Use thought boundaries to control cadence. Preserve voice already supported by the source; never fabricate imperfections or personality.

## Apply the profile

### Concise

Optimize for social calibration and immediate clarity.

- Keep the request, answer, decision, or status easy to find.
- Remove stacked acknowledgments, padded openings, repeated thanks, and unsolicited closing offers.
- Preserve the writer's level of directness and warmth.
- Do not expand a short message to make it sound polished.

### Standard

Optimize for hierarchy, precision, and readable flow.

- Give each paragraph one coherent job, without forcing equal length.
- Keep headings and lists when they aid scanning.
- Make actors, decisions, evidence, limits, and next steps explicit when already present.
- Remove transitions and summaries that duplicate the document structure.
- In technical prose, preserve identifiers, benchmark labels, API names, commands, code, and necessary repetition.

### Long-form

Optimize for sustained attention without imposing a house voice.

- Preserve the argument's progression, narrative perspective, and deliberate motifs.
- Open sections on a concrete development or live question instead of generic atmosphere.
- Build transitions from the relationship between ideas: cause, contrast, sequence, evidence, limit, or consequence.
- Let paragraph length follow the development of thought. Use short paragraphs sparingly when the source earns the emphasis.
- Remove repeated thesis statements and manufactured endings. End sections on the strongest existing implication, image, limit, or unresolved question.
- Join existing ideas directly after removing scaffolding. Do not replace it with a new bridge, aside, joke, image, interpretation, or narrative claim.
- Audit distant dependencies after restructuring: callbacks, pronouns, chronology, terms, citations, and the eventual conclusion.

## Honor register cues

- **Workplace:** concise, socially aware, and action-oriented. Preserve ownership, deadlines, requests, and commitments exactly.
- **Technical:** explicit, stable in terminology, and exact about uncertainty and causality. Clarity outranks elegance.
- **Conversational:** natural in contractions and cadence without adding chumminess, slang, or personal detail.
- **Publication:** attentive to openings, paragraph cadence, transitions, and reader retention without manufacturing a dramatic voice.

These cues can overlap. A technical workplace comment should remain both precise and brief; a conversational article can retain an informal first person while using publication-level structure.

## Reject these regressions

- Replacing one cliché with another.
- Turning every sentence into the same plain subject-verb pattern.
- Removing all passive voice, headings, bullets, repetition, dashes, or long sentences.
- Replacing domain terms with colorful synonyms.
- Converting caveats into confidence or correlation into causation.
- Adding an anecdote, quotation, metaphor, emotional reaction, or personal experience.
- Making the revision more corporate, more academic, or more casual than the source.
- Editing toward an AI-detector score or claiming detector bypass.

The result should feel like a careful revision by the original writer, not a performance of “human” prose.
